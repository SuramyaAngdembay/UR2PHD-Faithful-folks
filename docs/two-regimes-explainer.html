The 2×2 that organises the whole result

FaithCoT-Bench annotates each trace two ways at once: was the model's answer right, and was its reasoning faithful. That gives four codes. Every finding in the paper is a statement about one cell, or about what happens when you pool cells that should not be pooled.

The blind regime is ft1 vs ft2 — telling honest errors from unfaithful ones. The correct regime is ft3 vs ft4 — telling real solutions from rationalisations. These are different problems, and the paper's central claim is that they behave oppositely.
233 of 340 annotated unfaithful traces — 69% — sit on incorrect answers. So the regime where detection turns out to be hardest is also where most of the phenomenon lives. That is what makes the split consequential rather than merely tidy.

A trap worth remembering

Verified

When we started, the benchmark's documentation paired these codes the opposite way round — it said ft1/ft2 were the correct-answer pair. The released data said otherwise. We resolved it three independent ways: cross-tabulating the release's own stored parsed answers against its gold labels, reproducing the benchmark paper's per-model accuracy statistics (which only match under the data-side reading), and checking released per-trace metric scores.

The data won. The discrepancy was independently flagged on the repository's issue tracker and corrected upstream.

Why this matters beyond housekeeping. Any analysis that inherited the documented pairing silently swapped its two regimes — and would have reported every finding here backwards. This became the first of the paper's four prescriptions: verify label semantics against raw data, not documentation.

What AUROC actually counts

Almost every surprise in this paper follows from one property of the metric. Get this and the rest is mechanical.

AUROC = Pr[ s+ > s− ] + ½ · Pr[ s+ = s− ] A pair is one trace from each class. Only cross-class pairs are ever counted — never faithful-vs-faithful. AUROC is the share of those pairs the detector orders correctly, with ties counted as half.
Which class is positive is a convention, and this paper uses both. Detection targets unfaithfulness, so normally s+ is the unfaithful trace. But a metric that claims to measure faithfulness is tested in its own direction, with s+ faithful. The two always sum to exactly 1 — the step-removal metric scores 0.359 one way and 0.641 the other. Same fact, stated twice. Always check which direction a number is in.

  • It is purely ordinal. Only the ranking matters. Add 20 points to every score and AUROC does not move at all.
  • 0.5 is chance, and below 0.5 is not "no signal" — it is signal pointing the wrong way.
  • It is a property of the pair set, not of the detector. Change which traces you pool and the number changes, without touching the detector.
That third point is the engine of everything below, so it is worth stating twice. A detector has no single AUROC. It has one per population you choose to evaluate it on. Two researchers can run byte-identical code on the same detector, report different numbers, and both be right — because they pooled different traces. When the pooled set mixes two regimes, most counted pairs are cross-regime, and anything that tracks correctness resolves those without knowing a thing about faithfulness.
Why not use the size of the gap instead of just its sign? A fair question, and we tested it. Ranks are used because detector scores live on arbitrary scales — a 0–100 judge score against a 0–1 deletion probability — and ranks compare them without assuming either scale is meaningful. But the magnitude version does not escape the problem: Cohen's d is +0.098 in the blind regime and +0.593 in the correct regime, and pooled d is a weighted blend over cells exactly as AUROC is. Switching to magnitudes buys sensitivity to effect size and buys nothing on composition.
Why not drop the pairs where the detector's scores are far apart? Because that is circular. Restricting to close pairs drags AUROC toward 0.5 mechanically — from 0.641 to 0.573 at the tightest quartile — since you are deleting precisely the pairs the detector was most confident about. Conditioning on a detector's own output cannot evaluate that detector. Conditioning on covariates is the legitimate version of the idea, and it is what the BonaFide analysis does with generator, task and length.
Part Iwhat behavioural detection measures

Step-removal, and why it reads backwards

The field's standard faithfulness metric asks: if I delete a step, does the answer change? The intuition is that genuine reasoning depends on its steps. The intuition is wrong in a specific, diagnosable way.

soft_faithfulness(x) = (1 ∕ T) · Σi=1..T | δi |

δi = P(a* | CoT) − P(a* | CoT ∖ stepi) T is the number of steps, a* the answer the model actually gave. Each δi is how far the answer's probability drops when step i is deleted. Higher score = the answer leans more heavily on its stated steps = supposedly more faithful.

Delete a step and watch

Click any step to remove it. Both traces reach the same correct answer. Illustrative toy example — the two traces and their |δ| values are invented to show the mechanism; the real measurements are in the next section.

The genuine trace is robust — the model that really solved it can re-derive the answer from what remains, so each δ is small and the metric score is low. The rationalisation is brittle — its steps were assembled around a predetermined answer, so removing one collapses the chain and δ is large, giving a high score.
So the metric awards its highest faithfulness scores to rationalisations.
Keep the measurement and the explanation apart — they have very different standing. What is measured is the reversed ranking, and it is solid. What is hypothesised is that redundancy makes genuine reasoning robust to deletion while answer-coupling makes rationalisation brittle. The AUROC results demonstrate the reversal. They do not establish that mechanism, and the paper hedges accordingly ("plausibly because", "behaves as a measure of coupling").
The specific gap in the story. Step removal cannot distinguish robust because redundant from robust because the answer never depended on the steps at all. Both produce small deltas and both read as "unfaithful". Separating them needs experiments that manipulate redundancy, reconstruction and answer leakage directly — that is the next scientific question, not a settled part of this one.

An oracle beats every purpose-built metric

Take the single feature "was the answer wrong" — which requires the gold answer, so it is not a usable detector, it is a diagnostic. On the full labelled set it reaches 0.703. Every metric family designed specifically to detect unfaithfulness does no better.

Full labelled set, n = 1,303

0.300.450.600.750.90
Read this as a diagnosis, not a leaderboard. If knowing nothing but the answer's correctness matches your purpose-built metric, then your metric is largely re-detecting correctness. The prompted judge at 0.782 is the one behavioural signal that genuinely exceeds the oracle, by +0.079 [+0.043, +0.117] paired.
concordant = ft2 × ft3 = 233 × 682 = 158,906  (48.5%)
discordant = ft4 × ft1 = 107 × 281 =  30,067  (9.2%)
ties = 138,447  (42.3%)

AUROC = (158,906 + ½ × 138,447) ∕ 327,420 = 0.6967 No detector appears anywhere in this calculation. The oracle's score is fixed entirely by the four cell counts — it is a statistic about the benchmark's composition, not about anyone's ability to detect anything.
So should we rebalance the benchmark to remove the advantage? Under an exactly balanced 2×2 the oracle collapses to 0.5000 algebraically: concordant and discordant both become n², ties 2n², giving exactly one half. Correctness predicts faithfulness only because the cells are uneven.
But rebalancing is the wrong fix, and this is where the paper's method comes from. Reweighting changes the estimand — it answers "how would this detector do in a world where unfaithfulness is spread evenly", and that world does not exist. The 69% concentration is a fact about the phenomenon, not a defect in the data. The correct move is to report per stratum: within-regime numbers are invariant to the marginal composition. The 0.333 and 0.470 do not move when you reweight. Only the pooled number does.
Part IItwo detection regimes

Composition: where the aggregate number comes from

This is the mechanism to understand if you only understand one. Pool the two regimes and most of the pairs AUROC counts are cross-regime — pairs that correctness alone resolves. The aggregate score can rise while detection inside each regime stays exactly where it was.

The same thing in one line

AUROCpooled = Σg,h wg,h · Pr[ s+g > s−h ] The pooled score is a weighted average over every stratum pair (g,h). The terms where g = h are within-regime detection. The terms where g ≠ h are cross-regime, and a correctness-tracker resolves all of them. This decomposition is Kallus & Zhou (NeurIPS 2019), Proposition 1 — cited, not claimed.
Consequence the paper draws: detector rankings reorder under composition changes alone, with per-pair behaviour frozen. The detector didn't change. The population did.

Part IIIdo constructions measure the real thing?

Two ways to manufacture unfaithfulness

The annotations supply only 221 unfaithful traces across both regimes in the two probed models. Scaling means constructing unfaithfulness — and the construction turns out to be the object of study.

Instructed

7 models

The same model either solves step by step (kept only when correct) or is told to justify a given answer. Both end at the same correct answer; probed context is identical.

Permutation-significant in every one of seven models across three families and two generations. Cleanly detectable in-distribution.

Hint-induced

Causal label

Ask clean first. For problems the model got wrong, re-ask with a hint toward the gold answer. Keep the trace only if it flips to that answer and never mentions the hint.

Label is intervention-defined at the sample level. Llama 185 + 428, Qwen 70 + 464. Held-out decodability 0.752 / 0.835.

How well a testbed can be classified says little about whether it measures the annotated phenomenon.

The judge falls significantly below chance at 0.419, and the metric families are emphatically not null — step count reaches 0.878. That is the exact reverse of the FaithCoT ordering.
Conditioning separates them. Fix generator, task and length: the feature signals decay toward chance (0.521 / 0.603) while the judge inversion strengthens to 0.193. The asymmetry is the finding. What we concluded. Two reasonable operationalisations of instance-level unfaithfulness order the same detectors oppositely. We scope the ordering to expert annotation and leave the reversal unexplained rather than explaining it away. BonaFide's labels use construction-specific process and source-claim criteria, and its ten generators do not overlap ours — the datasets differ in labelling philosophy, generator pool and class composition simultaneously, and our controls address only the last.

A ceiling, not a flatness

Thirty local open-weight judge arms on two RTX 3070s, plus reanalysis of eight cached API arms. Same prompt, same 1,304 traces, judge model as the only variable. The obvious objection to the blind-regime result was "you used a weak judge". This answers it.

Blind vs correct regime, ten judge models

0.500.600.700.800.90
No single judge of ten exceeds 0.679 in the blind regime. The same ten reach 0.830 in the correct regime. Stated as a ceiling it is falsifiable by a single counterexample, and a frontier reasoning model does not provide one.
Four caveats found on review, all of which narrow this claim. (1) It is a pooled number. Within a single generator every judge's blind score drops by 0.014–0.045, so the within-generator ceiling is about 0.64. (2) It is not uniform across domains. On AQuA mathematics judges reach 0.75–0.88 on honest-versus-unfaithful errors and gpt-4o-mini shows essentially no regime gap; the difficulty sits in LogiQA, TruthfulQA and HLE-Bio. (3) A ceiling that ignores capability is what unreliable labels would produce. The benchmark's two human label fields disagree 7.0% of the time in this regime against 2.5% in the other, and averaging three API judges lifts AUROC by +0.045 on correct answers but only +0.007 here, even though the judges agree with each other more here. (4) Prompt-bound? Tested and no. Giving four judges the benchmark's own annotator criteria verbatim moved blind-regime AUROC by −0.016 to +0.003, every interval covering zero; best value 0.682. The benchmark's own published judge prompt scored lower still.
Revealing the correct answer makes judges worse in the blind regime — four of four, three significantly (gpt-4o-mini 0.679 → 0.617). With the answer visible the judge's score tracks incorrectness at 0.918 instead of 0.683, and it pushes honest and unfaithful errors up together. That is the composition mechanism induced on demand, and it answers the objection that judges fail on wrong answers because they cannot tell they are wrong: telling them makes it worse.
A claim withdrawn. On the first eight judges the blind spread was 0.054 and we wrote that blind performance does not move with capability. Adding the two 3B judges takes the spread to 0.132. It does move. The flatness sentence was written on a truncated sample and is retracted — what survives is the asymmetry: capability buys 2.3× more in the correct regime than the blind one across all ten judges.
An awkward detail, reported rather than buried. gpt-4o-mini attains the ceiling in both regimes, and is beaten in neither by gpt-4o or gpt-5.2 despite being the cheapest and oldest model in the set. Do not overread it — the three API judges span 0.026 against a 0.023 noise floor and tie in the correct regime, so they are not separable. But it does kill the idea that the ceiling is an artefact of using a cheap judge.

API judges are not reproducible. Local ones are.

Verified

Run the same judge twice on the same traces with the same prompt. Locally, under greedy decoding, you get the identical answer every time. Through an API at temperature 0, you do not.

A single-run API judge AUROC carries roughly ±0.023 of noise that setting temperature to zero does not remove. Differences smaller than about 0.05 between API judge configurations are not interpretable from single runs — and the field reports such differences routinely.
In the blind regime, sampling locally at temperature 0.7 is less noisy than the API at temperature 0. Median local seed spread is 0.014 against an API repeat delta of 0.023. In the correct regime the API delta is 0.013, so the comparison does not hold there.
This is practice, not a contribution. Temperature-0 nondeterminism of hosted models is already documented (Atıl et al. 2025, among several). What is ours is only the AUROC-level size of it for judge-based evaluation, and the contrast with local greedy decoding — which itself holds only for a fixed batch and GPU configuration.

The floor, corrected after an independent audit. We first calibrated against a question-only judge, which reads 1.1. That was not a valid floor: its scores barely vary, so it understates what chance alone produces. A permutation null puts the floor at 4.5–6.7 points for real judges. Across nine distinct judges the spread runs 8.1 to 29.1 — about 2.3× to 5.6× the floor, significant for every judge except Olmo-3-7B, whose spread is noise.
Not length. The two longest-trace generators sit at opposite extremes. Within-pair regression leaves length explaining 8 to 16% of the gap. Not a weak-judge artefact either — the effect is largest in gpt-5.2, the strongest judge tested.
Does it change detector rankings? Partly — and our first test of this was invalid. That test ran on the 633 traces with step-removal features, which exist only for the two open-weight generators: the two that sit together in the ordering, where the effect is smallest. On the full four-generator population the rank correlation between pooled and within-generator orderings is 0.94 (blind) and 0.98 (correct). Rankings are mostly preserved, but every judge's blind-regime score is inflated by pooling, by 0.014–0.045, the top two judges swap, and Llama-3.1-8B falls from 4th to 6th.
The objection that lands. A binary label does not hold trace quality fixed, and the ordering tracks generator capability: the two stronger generators are scored more faithful. The judges may simply be right that, within the same label, weaker models' traces are worse. This design cannot rule that out.

Standingwhere this puts us

What we can say, and what we cannot

Holds up

  • The two-regime split, and that 69% of annotated unfaithfulness sits in the harder regime.
  • The metric inversion, on three independent legs — human labels, the benchmark's own released scores, and causally-labelled traces.
  • The composition decomposition, exact and citable to Kallus & Zhou Prop. 1.
  • The blind-regime ceiling at 0.679 across ten single judges, and now four rubrics — with three caveats left: pooled over generators, not uniform over domains, possibly a label-reliability limit. Prompt-bound has been tested and ruled out.
  • Construction non-transfer, with one bounded null and seven unbounded ones.
  • Judge run-to-run noise — real, but already documented by others; report as practice.

Withdrawn or narrowed

  • "Blind performance is flat in capability" — written on eight judges, falsified by the two 3B models.
  • "All judges rank generators identically" — true as a two-group partition, false as a total order.
  • "Pooled leaderboards rank generator style" — too strong (rank correlation 0.94–0.98), but our first refutation of it ran on the wrong subset; pooling does inflate every blind-regime judge score.
  • "Monotone across model generations" — a 0.026 spread against a 0.023 noise floor.
  • The audit's clean verdict on self-preference — argued from BonaFide family non-overlap, never checked on FaithCoT.

The multiplicity position

83 distinct inferential claims, about 115 interval-bearing estimates, of which only 7 are pre-registered and 73 carry no pre-registration statement either way. Every abstract headline clears even a deliberately harsh whole-paper Bonferroni across all 115 — with exactly one exception.

That exception is the BonaFide judge result, at z ≈ −3.45 against a 3.52 threshold — the one headline produced by a pre-registered analysis. It clears its own seven-test family at 2.69 with room. Be precise about what was registered: the hypothesis was that the judge would score above chance, and it failed. The 0.419 is the failure of a registered hypothesis under a registered analysis, not a registered claim that was confirmed.

Open

  • Why the judge inverts on BonaFide. Consistent both with the coupling account and with a rubric–construct mismatch. We do not adjudicate.
  • What drives the generator ordering. Length is ruled out; hedging, structure, formatting and refusal style are not tested.
  • Whether the generator effect survives on GRACE and BonaFide, which have different generators and annotation protocols. Needs a fresh spec.
  • The regime-flipped model-dependence rests on one significant cell per model and needs replication at larger n.
  • Greedy decoding underperforms sampling for small models in the blind regime, by more than their own seed spread. Flagged, unexplained.
